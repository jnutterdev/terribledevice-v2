The Imagined City:
How Cyberpunk Became
Its Own Nostalgia

Forty years after Neuromancer, the genre that once predicted the future now mourns it — and the most interesting work is happening in the gap between what was promised and what arrived.

Author
M. VOSS
Published
2026-03-28
Pillar
SCREEN
Length
4,200 WDS // 18 MIN

There's a moment in Blade Runner 2049 where K stands in the ruins of Las Vegas — amber light, dead bees, Sinatra on a broken jukebox — and you realise the film isn't really about the future at all. It's a film about what it feels like to arrive somewhere too late. Cyberpunk, it turns out, has been making that film for a while now.

The genre was always about extrapolation — take the present, turn the dial, see what breaks. William Gibson's Neuromancer (1984) gave us the console cowboy jacking into the matrix, the megacorporations, the neon-soaked sprawl. Ridley Scott's Blade Runner arrived two years earlier and handed us the definitive visual grammar: rain-slicked streets, advertisement blimps, a city so dense it had forgotten it had a sky. These weren't predictions so much as pressure tests — what happens to people when the systems they live inside become too large, too fast, too indifferent to notice them?

The interesting thing is that the systems arrived. Not exactly as described, but close enough to be uncomfortable. The megacorporations are real. The surveillance infrastructure is real. The information overload, the erosion of privacy, the sense that the city runs on logic you're not authorised to read — all real. What didn't arrive was the aesthetic. Nobody got the trenchcoats and the rain. What we got instead was something flatter, more banal, and in its own way more unsettling.

The future arrived, just without the decor. And somehow that's the more frightening version.
— The Imagined City, §01

So the genre adapted. Or tried to. The cyberpunk of the 2010s and 2020s is defined less by extrapolation than by elegy — a mourning for the future that was supposed to come. Ghost in the Shell (2017), for all its problems, is interesting precisely because it's a remake that can't decide whether it's updating the source material or preserving it in amber. Cyberpunk 2077 is a game obsessed with the iconography of the genre's own past — Akira, Neuromancer, early Warp Records sleeve art — to the point where Night City feels less like a city and more like a mood board.

The Las Vegas sequence. Warm amber replaces the cold blue of the original — a deliberate inversion that reads as a genre reckoning.

This isn't necessarily a failure. Nostalgia is a legitimate mode — hauntology, as Mark Fisher argued, is what happens when a culture loses its ability to imagine a future that isn't recycled from the past. The question isn't whether cyberpunk is nostalgic. It's whether it knows it is.

The City That Wasn't
§ 02

What's genuinely strange about the cyberpunk city — Neo-Tokyo, Night City, Midgar, the Sprawl — is that it functions as both setting and argument. The city isn't where the story happens. The city is the story. Its density, its layering, its indifference to the individual moving through it — these aren't background details. They're the engine.

Akira (1988) understood this instinctively. Neo-Tokyo doesn't just contain Kaneda and Tetsuo's story — it produces it. The city is a system under so much pressure that it generates its own catastrophes. The government can't control Tetsuo because the government can't control Neo-Tokyo, and Neo-Tokyo can't control itself. The scale is the point. When something goes wrong at that scale, it doesn't go wrong in a small, containable way.

// Cross-reference: RoboCop (Verhoeven, 1987) — Delta City as the same argument, played for satire. Old Detroit as body, New Detroit as brand.
Akira's Neo-Tokyo doesn't just contain the story — it generates it. The city is a system under so much pressure that catastrophe becomes inevitable.
— The Imagined City, §02

The films and games that followed largely inherited this logic without always understanding it. Night City in Cyberpunk 2077 is extraordinarily dense, extraordinarily detailed, and — underneath all of it — surprisingly inert. It's a city that looks like it should be generating chaos at every level, but somehow the chaos is always scripted, always containable, always in service of the main quest. The pressure isn't real. The system doesn't push back.

The best cyberpunk cities — the ones that still feel vital — are the ones where the logic of the place has genuine consequences. Where the city isn't just atmosphere. Where you can feel the pressure.

What's Actually Good Right Now
§ 03

The most interesting cyberpunk work happening right now tends to be the stuff operating at the genre's margins — smaller, stranger, less concerned with proving its credentials. Disco Elysium is, among other things, a cyberpunk game that doesn't announce itself as one. The city of Revachol is a post-revolutionary ruin, its politics encoded into its architecture, its economics legible in its streets. The failure state is the point. You play a detective who might not survive the case, in a city that might not survive the century, and somehow the game makes you care about both.

On the music side, it's worth noting that the most vital work in the genre's sonic neighbourhood — Perturbator, Mega Drive, Haujobb, Forma Tadre — isn't trying to predict anything. It's building habitats. Environments you can live inside for forty minutes. The future as a place you might want to visit, even if you wouldn't want to stay.

That might be where cyberpunk ends up, actually. Not prediction, not elegy, but habitat. A set of conditions in which certain kinds of stories make sense. The genre as infrastructure rather than argument.

There are worse things to become.
